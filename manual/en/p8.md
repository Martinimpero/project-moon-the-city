# PART VIII — RUNNING THE GAME

This Part is the GM's toolbox: how to structure a session, build an NPC in seconds, generate a Contract or an Abnormality on the fly, and keep every other system in this book running smoothly at the table.

## 1. Running a Session

**Before the session**

1. Review the PCs' Bonds, Desires, Fears, and Boundaries.
2. Choose one unresolved consequence from the previous session.
3. Prepare one immediate problem.
4. Prepare one or two NPCs.
5. Prepare one Contract, if the session needs one.

**Opening the session**

Run a **Standing Check** — thirty seconds per player: what is your character carrying into today, mechanically or emotionally (an open Bond, a filling Clock, rising Stress)?

**During the session**

1. Present the situation.
2. Ask the players what they do.
3. Call for a roll only when the outcome is uncertain and consequential (Part II §1).
4. Apply the result — every roll changes something.
5. Advance Clocks and relationships when appropriate.
6. Escalate consequences rather than forcing a predetermined plot.

**Closing the session**

Run a **Consequence Beat** — state, out loud, what ticked: which Clocks moved, which Bonds strained or grew, what the City noticed. This is the last scene of the session, not optional bookkeeping after the table breaks.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A session opens with a player noting their Fixer's Debt Bond with a lawyer is about to be called in — that becomes the session's spine without the GM needing a separate hook. It closes with the GM naming three things: the market street's Escalation Ladder ticked to step two, a Trauma didn't fire despite a close call, and a new Bond formed with Clerk Sorel.

## 2. Building an NPC

A speaking NPC needs exactly three things:

- **Want** — what they're actually trying to get, which may not be what they say.
- **Bond Hook** — what kind of Bond they're likely to form with a PC, and which direction it runs first (Part V §1).
- **One Detail** — a single specific, sensory or verbal tic that makes them memorable.
- **Alignment** (optional) — the Sin that colors them, for any NPC likely to be in a fight or an argument that matters (Part X §8).

Anything beyond these three is optional. For a minor, one-scene NPC, a Want alone is often enough — save the full template for anyone likely to recur.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Clerk Anwen Sorel. Want — to feel like her job matters to someone. Bond Hook — Affection, forming from the PC's side first, if they treat her like a person. Detail — she narrates every stamp she applies under her breath, like a small ritual.

## 3. Generating a Contract

Combine one item from each list:

- **Client** — an Office manager, a Wing insider, a Syndicate go-between, a stranger with no obvious affiliation.
- **Objective** — recover, deliver, investigate, protect, remove.
- **Complication Seed** — a lie in the brief, a rival crew after the same thing, a Bond the client didn't mention, an Abnormality involved.

Then slot the result into the five-part Contract template (Part V §4). Risk rating calibrates to Difficulty: Offer only Risks the Office Grade allows (Part V §7).

| Risk | Calibration |
|---|---|
| 1 | A clear, safe path and a forgiving Complication Clause. |
| 2 | One genuine unknown. |
| 3 | Guarantees at least one Social Conflict or Combat scene of real consequence, and must directly engage at least one PC's Bond, Desire, or Fear in a way that creates a meaningful choice or consequence. |
| 4 | Touches the psychological layer directly — a Trauma trigger or Hail Mary should be plausible, not just possible. |

**GM Advice.** At Risk 3 and above, the personal element has to change what the player can do, not just get mentioned in narration. "This reminds your character of their Burden" is not enough — the scene needs to put the Bond, Desire, or Fear somewhere the player has to actually choose around it, the way the example below does. Risk 1 and 2 carry no such requirement — not every job needs to be personal, and forcing it every time will exhaust the table faster than it deepens the game. Flag Risk 4 Contracts tonally at session zero, not mid-Contract — players should know this tier exists before it's sprung on them.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Client: Wing insider. Objective: investigate. Complication Seed: a Bond the client didn't mention. Risk 3. Result: a researcher hires the crew to look into a colleague's disappearance, without mentioning she's the colleague's ex-Affection Bond — the investigation is guaranteed to surface something that complicates the client relationship itself, not just the mystery.

## 4. Running the Psychological Systems

Call for a Trauma check only at the moment a character's stated Boundary is actually, concretely crossed in the fiction — not merely when something bad happens nearby. Keep each PC's Boundary written where you can see it, and check it as a normal part of narrating consequences, the same reflex as checking Difficulty before a roll.

**Pacing the Hail Mary.** Aim for roughly one Hail Mary per character per campaign: a Boundary moment for any given PC every six to ten sessions. Steady characters (Resolve 4 or more) rarely run low enough on their own, so let the player dig deep, or bring the Boundary into a scene after pressure has already cost them E.G.O. When a character carries a Scar, offer a Reckoning scene soon.

Let Stress rise in visible bursts tied to real dramatic moments — a Hail Mary, a repeated Trauma, a Distortion — rather than a steady drip. Offer a Reckoning scene whenever a player seems to be looking for one; waiting for a coherent emotional stopping point usually serves the table better than a strict trigger. And when a character is Unsteady or Empty, don't skip the Flashpoint — it's the moment their Vice gets to speak, and letting it pass quietly undersells exactly what the mechanic is for.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A PC's Boundary is "killing in cold blood." Mid-Combat, they're asked to execute a surrendered enemy under Syndicate pressure. The GM pauses the Exchange specifically here — not at the earlier blows that just caused ordinary Harm — because this is the concrete moment the Boundary is actually on the table.

## 5. Running Social Conflict and Clocks

State the three outcomes out loud, in one breath, before any Social Conflict roll: "full success gets you X, a partial costs you Y, failure means Z." If you can't fill in all three quickly, the scene isn't ready to be rolled — it needs more fictional grounding first.

**Clock calibration.**

| Size | Use for |
|---|---|
| 4 segments | Immediate, scene-level pressure — can fill within a single session if pushed hard. |
| 6 segments | Most faction Heat — spans a handful of sessions. |
| 8 segments | Campaign-length stakes, including the City-wide Notice Clock (Part VII §7). |

Fill exactly one segment per meaningful setback, never more than two even on a severe Complication, so the pace of escalation stays visible.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A GM unsure how fast to fill a new Syndicate's Heat Clock defaults to 6 segments, ticking it once per botched job or piece of exposed compromising information — by session four, the crew can see the Escalation Ladder's second step coming and choose whether to keep pushing.

## 6. Running Combat

**Example equipment**, by category (Part VI §5): Improvised — a broken bottle, a chair, bare hands. Light — a folding knife, a compact pistol, a length of pipe. Heavy — a shotgun, a length of rebar swung two-handed, a riot baton. Abnormality-touched — treat case by case, always with a Stress cost attached to use.

**Multi-front fights.** When a Threat group and a named opponent act in the same Exchange, resolve them as two separate participants within it — but never let a single Exchange run more than roughly one action per PC plus these two. If it's dragging, collapse minor beats into a single roll rather than resolving every mook's individual swing. When it's just a single dangerous named opponent against a full party, with no Threat group alongside them, remember their one additional defensive response (Part VI §6) — without it, a full party can end a "boss" fight in a single Exchange purely through numbers.

The Boundary-check habit applies exactly as hard inside Combat as anywhere else — a killing blow against someone's stated Boundary deserves the same pause an execution scene would get outside a fight.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A crew fighting both a lieutenant (named, full Harm track) and his three enforcers (a single Threat) resolves each Exchange as: PC actions in fictional order, one enforcer-Threat action, one lieutenant action — four "slots" total regardless of how many PCs are present.

## 7. Improvising an Abnormality

Combine one entry from each list:

- **Behavior** — mimics something familiar wrongly; counts or sorts obsessively; only acts when unobserved; responds to a specific word or name.
- **Trigger** — being touched; a lie spoken near it; a debt or promise being broken; being looked at directly.
- **Breach Category** — a Harm-equivalent physical effect; a Clock starts or jumps; a Trauma trigger with no physical Harm at all; something is taken — an object, a memory, a name.

Then assign an Approach (Part VII §6). Include a Resolve-led Instinct Approach where the Abnormality's danger is about willpower or endurance rather than cleverness or empathy — holding still, refusing to answer, outlasting its patience.

> **EXAMPLE**
> *The Second Knock.* Behavior: only acts when unobserved. Trigger: answering a knock at a door twice in one scene. Breach Category: a Trauma trigger — whoever answers finds the space behind the door isn't where it should be, no Harm roll, just a Trauma check if it hits a Boundary. Approach: Instinct, plain Resolve — simply refusing to answer a second knock, no matter how convincing it sounds, resolves the encounter cleanly.

## 8. Campaign Structures

Three starting shapes, any of which can shift into another over time. None require more than one District and one or two factions prepped before session one.

- **Episodic** — the crew works through their Association, one or two Contracts per session, City detail generated only as needed. Lowest prep, highest flexibility.
- **Institutional** — the campaign centers on one Wing, Syndicate, or Office in depth, building toward that institution's own Escalation Ladder or internal Secret. Only that one institution needs to be fully prepped.
- **Notice Arc** — a longer campaign explicitly building toward the City-wide Notice Clock (Part VII §7). Early sessions can be loose and episodic while the GM privately tracks how each session's choices feed the Clock.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A table starts Episodic with just one Association and one stretch of the Backstreets prepped. By session six, recurring friction with the Kurokumo Clan has organically shifted the campaign toward an Institutional shape without anyone declaring the change.

**GM Advice.** One District and one faction is genuinely enough to start. Resist the urge to finish building the City before session one.

## 9. Worked Example: A Full Session Zero Prep

Applying every tool in this Part, a GM preps a first session in under twenty minutes.

**District:** the Backstreets market street, already detailed with its Open War Clock.

**NPC:** Clerk Sorel, Want/Bond Hook/Detail as already established.

**Contract:** Client, stranger with no obvious affiliation; Objective, protect; Complication Seed, a rival crew after the same thing; Risk 2 — someone hires the party to protect a shipment moving through the Backstreets market street, not mentioning that the Kurokumo Clan already has eyes on it.

**Campaign shape:** Episodic, ready to bend toward Institutional if the table latches onto the Clan.

The session opens with the Standing Check: nobody has history yet, so the GM instead names what's already true about the City — the Open War Clock sits at two of six. Play generates a Social Conflict with a Kurokumo lieutenant, stakes declared per §5, which fails into a Partial, ticking the Clock to three. The session closes with the Consequence Beat: the Clock moved, a new adversarial Bond formed, and the shipment made it through — barely.

Nothing here required more than a single District and a single generated Contract to produce a full, consequence-driven first session.

## 10. The Downtime Phase

Between Contracts the table plays a **downtime phase**. Open it with the Consequence Beat (§1) and the Report (Part VII §3). Each character then gets **two downtime actions**, and rests for free (a full night returns 1 E.G.O., a downtime scene 1 more, to a maximum of 2 between Contracts: Part IV §1).

Pick two of:

- **Cope:** a healthy or unhealthy coping activity lowers Stress by 1 (Part IV §2).
- **Reckoning:** a played scene confronting the wound (Part IV §7).
- **Tend a Bond:** a scene that restores a spent point of Bond Strength or grows one by +1 (Part V §1).
- **Treat or heal:** treatment rolls and one tier of Harm healed (Part VI §2).
- **Train:** spend Marks on a Skill or Attribute, in a scene that shows it (Part IX §7).
- **Work the Office:** do the Office's errands for standing, a Resource, or a favor, or pay Resources into the crew's Fund (Part V §7).
- **Upkeep:** pay 1 Resources (rent, taxes, the Office's cut), and 1 more to repair Spent gear or maintain a Backstreets augmentation; skipping it starts a Clock or costs Stress +1.

The GM may also use downtime to advance a Clock (Part II §4) the PCs have ignored.

## 11. Session Zero and Safety

This game asks players to play through grief, fear, shame and loss, and to put a character's worst moments on the table. That works best when everyone chose to be there and knows how to stop. Hold a session zero before the first Contract.

**What the game contains.** Say it out loud: violence and death; grief and the loss of loved ones; guilt and trauma; coercion and abuse of power (Wings, Syndicates, Offices); addiction and unhealthy coping; body horror and mutilation (Harm, Distortion, Abnormalities); and loss of control over a character (Distortion). The City's numbness toward all of it is part of the tone.

**Lines and Veils.** Ask each person for **Lines** (things that will not appear in the game at all) and **Veils** (things that can happen but fade to black, described and not shown). Write them where everyone can see them. Nobody has to explain a Line.

**The tools.** Agree to at least these, and use them without comment. They are community tools; look up their originators (the X-Card is John Stavropoulos's, Lines and Veils is Ron Edwards's) if you want more.

- **The X-Card.** An index card with an X on it, in the middle of the table. Anyone may tap it, with no explanation, to cut or change what is happening. The GM edits the scene and moves on.
- **Pause.** Anyone may say "pause" to stop the game for a breath or a check-in. The game resumes when they say so.
- **Rewind and fast-forward.** Anyone may ask to go back to before something happened, or to skip past it.
- **Stars and Wishes.** At the end of a session each player names a *star* (something that worked) and a *wish* (something they'd like more of).

**Session zero checklist.**

1. **Tone.** Flag Risk 4 Contracts and Distortion before they happen (Part VIII §3).
2. **Lines, Veils and tools** as above.
3. **Characters.** The Burden is a concrete past event, but it does not have to be a graphic one. Write it at the level of detail the player wants to put on the table: the table needs a trigger, not the worst version. The GM does not push for more.
4. **The crew.** Office, Crew Bond, and how the PCs know each other (Part V §7).
5. **The Sins.** Alignment and what it says about the Wound (Part X §2).
6. **Lethality and Fallen.** Tell the table that whether a character dies is the player's call (Part IV §6), and that the GM never kills a character by fiat.
7. **Table rules.** Conflict between PCs is a scene and a Bond, not a betrayal of the table. Ask everyone: *what would make this game not fun for you?*
8. **The era** and how rare E.G.O. is (Part I §2).

**During play.**

- **Distortion.** Before the GM speaks or acts for a Distorted character, ask the player whether they want to play it themselves, hand it to the GM, or share it. The player can take the character back at any time.
- **The Verdict.** Check the words with the player beforehand: they should hit the character's Fear, not the person. If the player flinches, soften them.
- **Trauma beats.** Players narrate their characters' reactions (Part IV §3). Anyone may choose to narrate a reaction off-screen instead.
- **Heavy scenes.** After a Hail Mary or a Distortion, end the scene and take a breath before the next one.
- **If someone is off.** Stop. Ask in private. Offer a break, a change, or an exit. Believe them. Resume only when they are ready.

**After the session.** Run Stars and Wishes. If a Line came close, say so, and adjust the Lines.
