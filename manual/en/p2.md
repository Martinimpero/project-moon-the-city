# PART II — PLAYING THE GAME

## 1. The Core Roll

Whenever a character attempts something uncertain and consequential, roll dice.

**Build the pool.** Add one relevant Attribute and one relevant Skill together — the total is the number of ten-sided dice (d10) you roll. A character with Presence 3 and Persuasion 2 rolls 5 dice. If a character has no dots in the relevant Skill, roll the Attribute alone. A roll may also be **tagged** with a Sin the character has rating in, which can add or remove dice (Part X §3).

**Count Successes.** Each die that shows 7, 8, 9, or 10 is a Success. Everything else counts for nothing — there's no partial credit per die, only whole Successes.

**Compare to Difficulty.** The GM sets a Difficulty — the number of Successes needed — before the dice are rolled. Difficulty is almost always 1 to 3:

| Difficulty | What it represents |
|---|---|
| 1 | Routine, but not risk-free — a task complicated by pressure, time, or unfamiliarity. |
| 2 | Genuinely challenging, professional-grade difficulty. |
| 3 | The City actively pushing back — dangerous, expert-level, or opposed by someone competent. |
| 4+ | Reserved for the exceptional. Realistically needs preparation, Help, or spent E.G.O. to be achievable at all. |

**GM Advice.** Say the Difficulty out loud before the dice are rolled, every time. The player should know what they're risking before they commit to it.

## 2. Reading the Result

Compare the number of Successes rolled to the Difficulty:

- **Successes ≥ Difficulty + 2 — Critical Success.** Full effect, plus something extra: a bonus, free information, no cost, a Bond strengthens, time saved.
- **Successes ≥ Difficulty — Success.** The character gets what they were after.
- **Successes = Difficulty − 1 — Partial.** The GM narrates one of two outcomes, whichever fits the fiction: "you succeed, but—" (the character gets it, at a cost) or "you fail, and—" (the character misses it, and the situation gets worse).
- **Successes ≤ Difficulty − 2 — Failure.** The attempt doesn't work, and the scene moves forward with a real consequence. Failure is never "nothing happens."
- **Zero Successes with at least one die showing a 1 — Critical Failure.** Something actively goes wrong, beyond ordinary failure. Check for this first: it overrides Partial and Failure at any Difficulty, and a 1 on any die counts, E.G.O. dice included. Use it rarely, for moments that deserve it.

At Difficulty 1 there is no Failure band (it would need −1 Successes), so the worst ordinary result is a Partial.

**Opposed rolls.** When another character is actively resisting — in a negotiation, a chase, a fight — both sides build a pool and roll. Compare total Successes directly, from the point of view of whoever is trying to change the situation (the attacker, the one pushing, the one acting): a margin of **+3 or more** is a Critical Success, **+1 or +2** a Success, **0 or −1** a Partial, and **−2 or worse** a Failure. A tie therefore favors whoever isn't the one trying to change the situation — the defender, the incumbent, the person not acting — who holds, while the pusher gets only a Partial. Critical Failure still checks each side's own roll.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A researcher (Mind 3, Investigation 2 — five dice) is trying to find out what's actually being tested on the floor above her. The GM sets Difficulty 2: the records are real, but deliberately obscured. She rolls 8, 6, 9, 3, 2 — two dice show 7+, so two Successes, meeting Difficulty exactly. Success: she finds what she was looking for, cleanly, with no complication attached.

## 3. Helping

A character can help another character's roll instead of rolling for their own outcome.

To help, the helping player describes what their character is doing to support the attempt, then rolls their own Attribute + Skill pool at Difficulty 1. Each Success on that roll adds one bonus die to the lead character's pool before it's rolled. A character can only be helped this way once per roll, and helping puts the helper in the scene — if it goes wrong, they're exposed to the same consequence as the character they're helping.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Two Fixers are trying to talk their way past a checkpoint. One leads the roll (Presence + Persuasion); the other backs the story (Mind + Deception, Difficulty 1). The second player rolls two Successes, adding two bonus dice to the lead roll before it's made.

## 4. Clocks

Not everything resolves on a single roll. When pressure should build over multiple scenes — an investigation closing in, a faction's suspicion growing, a countdown to something arriving — track it on a **Clock**.

A Clock is a circle divided into 4, 6, or 8 segments. Choose the size based on how fast the pressure should build: 4 for something that can boil over within a single session if pushed hard, 6 for pressure that spans a handful of sessions, 8 for something campaign-length.

Fill one segment per meaningful setback — a botched roll, a Complication, a pattern of behavior the fiction makes notice-worthy. Never fill more than two segments even on a severe failure, so the pace of escalation stays visible to the players. When a Clock fills completely, something concrete happens — never vague. An audit. A visit. A contract quietly pulled. A Syndicate enforcer at the door. A full Clock is not automatically a disaster; it's the story escalating in a specific, prepared direction.

A character or crew can run more than one Clock at a time — separate Heat with a Wing and a Syndicate, for instance — and should.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A Fixer crew working against a Wing's interests is accumulating Heat on a 6-segment Clock: two segments from a botched extraction, one from a triggered Complication Clause, one from a loose-lipped contact. At four of six, the GM has Wing security start showing up a beat early to nearby jobs — not the full response yet, but a clear signal the Clock is filling.

## 5. Consequences

Every roll that isn't a clean Success should change something about the situation — a Clock ticks, a Bond strains, an NPC notices, a Complication lands. The game has no rule that produces "you roll, and nothing happens." If a roll wouldn't change anything regardless of outcome, don't call for it — just narrate the result and move on.

**GM Advice.** Before any consequential roll, state what a full Success gets, what a Partial costs, and what a Failure changes — out loud, in one breath, before the dice are picked up. If you can't fill in all three quickly, the scene isn't ready to be rolled yet; it needs more fictional grounding first.
