# PART IV — THE SELF

Two numbers track what the City costs a character: E.G.O., spent scene to scene, and Stress, carried across the whole campaign. This Part covers both, along with Trauma, the Hail Mary, E.G.O. Manifestation, Distortion, and the Reckoning — what happens when a character is pushed past what they can carry, and how that pressure stops being abstract and becomes something you can actually see.

## 1. E.G.O.

E.G.O. is a pool, rated 0 to a character's Resolve score, representing how much of their own identity a character can consciously draw on right now. A character wants to keep it full. Every point spent is a real piece of themselves, given up on purpose — and the less of it they have left, the less steady they are.

**Recovery.** A full night's rest returns 1 E.G.O. A genuine downtime scene — real time spent on something other than the current Contract — returns 1 more. A character can't recover more than 2 E.G.O. this way between Contracts.

**Spending E.G.O.** Before rolling, a player may spend E.G.O. points one-for-one as extra d10s, added to the pool in a different color. E.G.O. dice count toward Successes like any other die.

Any E.G.O. die that shows a 1 or 2 generates a **Complication** — a concrete cost the GM drops into the fiction, win or lose: a Bond strains, a Wing takes notice, evidence gets left behind, an old wound flares. On a roll tagged with a Sin, it is that Sin's Complication instead (Part X §5).

E.G.O. can also be taken rather than spent. An interrogation, a targeted psychological attack, a betrayal, or a sufficiently horrific Abnormality encounter can force a loss directly, as a scripted cost or the result of an opposed roll. At Stress 2 or higher, a forced loss like this takes one extra point beyond whatever's scripted; at Stress 4 or higher, it takes two extra. Stress doesn't just shrink what a character can hold — it makes the City better at taking what's left.

**Running low.** Once current E.G.O. drops **below half** its maximum, judged at the start of a roll (before any E.G.O. dice are spent on it), a character is **Unsteady**: the first die that shows a 1 in any roll each scene triggers a **Flashpoint** — the character's Vice or worst instinct takes the wheel for a beat, whether the roll itself succeeded or not. The GM narrates the beat; the player still plays the character through it, just from underneath something they didn't choose. When a Flashpoint fires, it is the Vice or the Urge of the character's Sin (Part X §5).

At 0 E.G.O., a character is **Empty**: every roll that shows a 1 triggers a Flashpoint (one per roll, however many 1s), not just the first of the scene. A character whose maximum is 2 or less skips Unsteady and goes straight from steady to Empty. The less of themselves is left, the less of it stays theirs.

**The Voice.** Anyone pushed hard enough hears it: a quiet voice that only they hear, offering a reading of their life. Characters begin to hear it when they become Unsteady, and louder when Empty. It is not a rule, it is a tone: when a Flashpoint hits, the Vice is the voice speaking through them, and its shape follows the character's highest Attribute. **Body** lashes out, **Mind** withdraws or sabotages quietly, **Presence** performs or provokes, **Resolve** refuses and digs in. At the Hail Mary, the Voice becomes the Verdict (below).

If E.G.O. would hit 0 at a moment that hits a character's Boundary directly, that's not simply Empty. That's a Hail Mary.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A Fixer is cornered and needs to talk her way out before it turns violent — Presence 2 + Persuasion 1, three dice, Difficulty 3. She spends 2 E.G.O., adding two dice to the pool: 8, 7, 9 base plus 2, 6 from E.G.O. — three Successes, clearing Difficulty 3. One E.G.O. die showed a 2, so one Complication: her voice cracked with something colder than intimidation, and someone in the room notices. She's down to 0 E.G.O. now — Empty for the rest of the scene. This wasn't a Boundary moment, so nothing worse happens, but she's rolling on a hair trigger until some E.G.O. comes back.

## 2. Stress

Stress is rated 0–5 and tracks how close a character's fault line is to breaking, across the whole campaign. Unlike E.G.O., it very rarely comes down.

**Raises Stress by 1:** a Trauma trigger firing, a stated Boundary getting violated, a Critical Failure, losing a Bond outright rather than merely spending it. An E.G.O. Manifestation also raises Stress by 1 — even winning costs something.

**Raises Stress by 2:** a Distortion.

**Lowers Stress.** Downtime spent on a healthy coping activity tied to a Bond — real rest, real time with someone who matters — lowers Stress by 1. An unhealthy version (drinking alone, picking a fight, working through the night instead of sleeping) also lowers it by 1, but strains a Bond in the process. Either version works at most once between Contracts. Beyond that, Stress only comes down through a Reckoning.

**Stress and E.G.O. maximum.**

| Stress | E.G.O. maximum |
|---|---|
| 0–1 | no change |
| 2–3 | −1 |
| 4–5 | −2, and **Fraying** |

E.G.O. maximum never drops below 1.

While Fraying, every Hail Mary roll takes a further +1 Difficulty.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A character spends an evening getting quietly, seriously drunk instead of calling the friend who's been trying to reach her. Stress drops by 1. Her Bond with that friend drops a point too, and next session he brings it up — the City took something back even during rest.

## 3. Trauma

When a Boundary or Fear a character doesn't already have a Trauma for gets hit hard enough, it creates one instead of just raising Stress on its own. Player and GM write it together, on the spot, as a one-line trigger condition.

Whenever that trigger condition happens again in the fiction, Stress rises by 1 and the player narrates a brief, specific reaction — a habit, a flinch, a thing they say or can't say. This is a requirement, not optional color.

A character can carry more than one Trauma; there's no hard cap.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Mira's Burden gave her a starting Trauma: told not to ask questions about a patient's condition, Stress rises automatically. Three sessions in, a Wing supervisor tells her to patch someone up and not log it. The trigger fires — Stress ticks up, and her player narrates her hands going very still for a second before she gets back to work.

**GM Advice.** Don't let a Trauma get waved off with "yeah, she's uncomfortable." The narration is what makes it land — if the table starts skipping it, treat that as a sign this Trauma is due for a Reckoning, not a rule to quietly drop.

**A second fault line.** When the same Trauma has fired three times, the player may write a **second Boundary** from it, with the GM's agreement: the line the character now won't cross because of how it keeps hurting. A character may hold up to three Boundaries over a campaign. Each is its own fault line: the Hail Mary uses whichever Boundary the moment hits, each can carry its own Scar, and each can earn its own Permanent E.G.O.

## 4. The Hail Mary

The Hail Mary triggers when a character's E.G.O. would hit 0 at a moment that hits their Boundary directly — not any time E.G.O. runs out, only when the fiction is already asking how much of themselves the character will give up. A player may also **dig deep**: at such a moment they can choose to trigger it even though E.G.O. wouldn't hit 0, by spending **all** their remaining E.G.O. (at least 2 points). It is how a steady character gives everything to one moment. Digging deep isn't available on a fault line that already carries an unhealed Scar.

**Rolling it.** Resolve + a relevant Skill — often Empathy, sometimes whatever the moment is actually about — Difficulty 2–3 depending on how deep the contradiction cuts. **Digging deep adds +1 Difficulty:** the character isn't at the brink, so they have to mean it. The roll is tagged with the Sin of the crisis (Part X §5).

**The Verdict.** At the moment the Hail Mary triggers, a voice only the character can hear tells them what their Wound has always said about them, in the words of their Fear ("You froze. People like you always do."). The GM speaks it. It should be true enough to hurt. The character's **Acceptance** is the answer: they deny the Verdict and say who they are underneath. An awakening isn't an escape from the Wound; it is getting past it by accepting who you are underneath. The roll shows whether it holds. A Success is the denial holding. If the player can't or won't say it in their own words, a Success or Critical Success becomes a Partial Distortion instead. It is not a formula, and the GM judges whether it was earned, as with a Reckoning. A Partial is half-believing the Verdict, and a Failure is the Verdict being louder than they are. The moment needs a **sincere Desire** the Boundary now contradicts; it can't be a tragedy the character already thought they deserved.

Before rolling, the player may spend a Bond to add a die. Each Boundary the character has already broken this campaign costs a further −1 die on this specific roll.

- **Critical Success — Manifestation, clean.** E.G.O. refills to 1. Stress +1.
- **Success — Manifestation, at a cost.** Same manifestation, but it forces an immediate hard price in the fiction — a Trauma marked, or a Boundary bent right there in the scene.
- **Partial — Partial Distortion.** A real, visible transformation — but the character stays close enough to themselves to keep playing through it.
- **Failure — Complete Distortion.** The self is pushed out; the Distortion's own wants take over, and the GM may need to direct the character until something in the fiction brings them back.
- **Critical Failure — Complete Distortion, already critical.** Same as above, but treat the crisis as already close to its worst — death or a permanent transformation should feel like live options from the first moment, not a distant threat.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A former Wing researcher has spent the campaign hiding what she did to get out of her contract. Cornered by someone who knows, her E.G.O. already at 1, the confrontation hits her Boundary directly — spending her last point would take her to 0. Hail Mary. Resolve 3 + Empathy 2, five dice, Difficulty 3. She hasn't broken a Boundary yet this campaign, so no penalty, and she spends a Bond with her one remaining friend for a sixth die. She rolls two Successes against Difficulty 3 — a Partial. Partial Distortion: the corners of her eyes go flat and mirror-bright, catching light like glass, and her voice drops into something layered underneath her own — but it's still her choosing the words, still her decision to finally say the thing she's spent years burying. Her handler recognizes her voice under it and keeps saying her name until the mirror-shine fades and she's shaking, fully herself again — but everyone in the room saw exactly what almost took over. Stress rises by 2.

## 5. E.G.O. Manifestation

The character meets the crisis as themselves — and it shows. Something of their identity becomes real enough to touch, and touchable things are physical: a weapon that wasn't there a moment ago, armor growing out of an old scar, eyes that catch light wrong, a voice that suddenly carries. E.G.O. Manifestation isn't a bonus wearing narration as a costume — it's the character's identity made momentarily solid, in whatever shape actually answers the crisis. The first time, it lasts for that scene only, and fades when the scene ends. Its look follows the character's Alignment (Part X §5).

If the same character Manifests successfully a second time on the same fault line — not necessarily consecutively, possibly sessions apart — it becomes a **Permanent E.G.O.** (Effloresced, the stable kind; the first Manifestation was Volatile): a named ability the player and GM design together on the spot, **with a physical form** (armor, a weapon, a mark, a tool, a place), always available rather than restricted to Hail Mary moments, costing 1 E.G.O. to activate like a supercharged Skill. It should be symbolically tied back to the character's Identity phrase, and it should look like something, not just do something. **A Permanent E.G.O. closes that fault line to Distortion:** a Hail Mary on it cannot become a Distortion. A failure costs E.G.O. down to 0 and Stress +2 instead. The one exception is a **drastic moment**: the GM may declare that the very thing the E.G.O. is built on is being broken (the person it was for is killed by the character's own choice, the Identity phrase proven a lie). Then the E.G.O. is tested for real: the Hail Mary is rolled at +1 Difficulty, and a Partial or worse Distorts as normal, the Permanent E.G.O. shattering with it.

**GM Advice.** Ask what it looks like before the dice have even settled. If a Manifestation only changes numbers, it isn't landing yet — a weapon, a mark, a change in the eyes, armor that fits like it grew there. It doesn't have to be beautiful, and it doesn't have to be big. It has to be visible.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Mira's first Manifestation shows up as a moment of impossibly steady hands — warmth runs down both her forearms and, for that scene, her hands simply stop shaking. If she Manifests a second time on the same fault line — the fear of freezing when someone she loves is dying — the table names her Permanent E.G.O. *Steady Hands*: that same warmth on command, and for 1 E.G.O. she can act with total, unerring precision in a medical or rescue crisis for one full scene, no roll required for the basic action, though the GM can still call for a roll on anything genuinely uncertain layered on top.

## 6. Distortion

The character surrenders to the crisis — and for a while, they become something else. The same pressure that fuels a Manifestation doesn't disappear when a character loses that argument with themselves; it still becomes physical. It just isn't theirs to shape anymore. Their Vice, their worst instinct, their most overwhelming desire takes the wheel and makes itself real — not a costume, not a debuff, an actual transformation. Body, appearance, behavior, capability: whatever the fiction supports, the GM plays it as real, physical, and dangerous, visible and interactable by everyone else at the table.

**How long it lasts is not a rule — it's a question.** A Distortion doesn't run on a timer. It lasts until the character is brought back to themselves, until they're killed, or until the fiction makes clear that this is who they are now. Sometimes that's a handful of tense exchanges, if someone reaches them fast. Sometimes it's the whole session. The dice that triggered it only decide how bad the crisis starts — the fiction decides how it ends.

**The person is still in there.** Neither severity is mindlessness. A character can Distort and still recognize their friends, still remember what they promised, still speak, still resist, still choose — right up until the moment (if it ever comes) that they genuinely can't anymore. The feeling to aim for is: *I know who you are. I know who I am. I cannot stop myself.*

- **Partial Distortion.** The character has really, physically become their Distortion — a mutation, extra limbs, an impossible or animalistic shape, some phenomenon manifesting off their skin, the room itself answering them. It is never just strange eyes or a changed voice; if it isn't visibly monstrous, it isn't a Partial Distortion yet. But the player keeps the wheel. They play the character as that thing, folding the Distortion's overwhelming impulse into every choice they make — this is "my character as a monster," not "my character with a penalty."
- **Complete Distortion.** The character has given up much more of themselves. The Distortion's own wants are now doing most of the driving, and the GM may need to speak and act for the character when the player genuinely can't find a way through as themselves (and should ask first: Part VIII §11) — but the player isn't benched. The character stays in the scene, and the person inside is still reachable: someone can appeal to a Bond, throw a Wound back in their face, name a Boundary they once held, physically grab them, or just refuse to let go. Complete Distortion should read as a confrontation the whole table gets pulled into, not a cutscene.

The exact shape of either state — what the transformation actually looks like — gets built on the spot from the character's Wound, Vice, Boundary, and whatever triggered the Hail Mary. A guardian who encloses everyone in the room to keep them "safe." A shape trailed by phantom versions of everyone who ever left, pulling anyone nearby into itself. A living monument that forces everyone present to look at what it did. There's no fixed catalog — the crisis and the character write the form.

**Recovery** is not a roll. There is no "recover from Distortion" check — a character comes back because something happens in the fiction. A Bond reaches them. Someone says the one true thing. They see the face of someone they failed, or remember a promise they made. Someone refuses to give up on them, or gets close enough to say their name and mean it. They're beaten, and the person who beat them chooses to save them instead of finishing it. The GM judges whether the scene actually did that work — these are examples of what can bring someone back, not a checklist to satisfy.

**Death** is a real possibility while a character is Distorted, through the same Harm rules that apply everywhere else in the game — see Part VI. If the character is killed before they find their way back, they die as what they'd become. That's the tension a Distortion should carry: can the people who love this person reach them before it's too late.

**Going permanent.** A Distortion can become permanent — the character genuinely doesn't come back — but that's a fictional outcome, never an automatic result of any roll. A Critical Failure only guarantees the crisis starts closer to the edge; it never guarantees the ending. If the fiction supports a permanent break, the player and GM decide together what that means: the character is retired, becomes an NPC, becomes a recurring monster the table might meet again, or the story simply ends here for them. The mechanics never take that choice away from the player.

Whatever happens while a character is lost to a Distortion, at either severity, should earn the word "monster" and give everyone else at the table something to do — talk them down, fight, restrain, investigate what triggered it, exploit what it's afraid of, risk themselves to reach the person inside. A Distortion is a scene the table plays through, not a condition applied to a character sheet.

**Running a Distortion: the starter kit.** A Distortion is played from the fiction, but a GM running one for the first time needs numbers. These are the defaults; change them freely.

- **Pool.** The Distorted character rolls what they would roll anyway, with their Sin's Fit at +2 / −3 and the Sin tagged at rating 4 (Part X §5). A roll the Distortion is *for* (smashing, binding, swallowing, drowning, commanding by presence, whatever its form does) adds **+2 dice**. A roll that needs the person (delicate work, lying, comforting) takes **−2 dice**.
- **Traits.** Give the Distortion traits that fit its form: **two** for a Partial Distortion, **three** for a Complete one. *Reach*: attacks at range or through walls. *Armor*: +1 die on defense. *Rend*: on a Success the target also takes a Complication (pinned, marked, disarmed). *Hold*: on a Success the target can't retreat until freed. *Presence*: Pressure rolls at +2, and anyone seeing it for the first time loses 1 E.G.O. *Spread*: it acts against a group as one Threat roll.
- **Harm.** Harm advances normally, and death is as real as ever. A Distorted body still feels it; Harm penalties apply.
- **Reaching them.** Recovery is not a roll, but three things count as a **real attempt**: an appeal to a Bond (the Bond is spent), naming the Boundary or the Burden, and delivering the Sin's Return (Part X). If the table wants a visible count, use a **Reach Clock**: 2 segments for a Partial Distortion, 3 for a Complete one. Each real attempt fills one; delivering the Return fills two. When it fills, the character comes back. A careless attempt (no Bond, no truth) fills nothing. The GM may bring the character back sooner if the scene has already done the work.
- **Duration.** A Partial Distortion normally ends within the scene if anyone reaches it. A Complete one lasts until reached or until the session ends. If the session ends with the character still Distorted, the next session opens in that scene.

Every Distortion that resolves — recovery, not death or Fallen — leaves exactly one **Scar**, written together on the spot:

- A **Tell** — a visible, narratable change in how the character presents to other people afterward, evidence of what happened.
- The Boundary that broke, marked **Broken** — on a Hail Mary that touches it, the character can no longer spend a Bond to add a die, and it also imposes a standing −1 die penalty on any roll that directly invokes it, until it Heals.

Distortion doesn't stack. A character can only Distort while there's still a human self on that fault line to give way — the first time it breaks is the Scar. If the Hail Mary triggers again on a fault line that's already carrying an unhealed Scar, there's little human left there to lose a second time. A **Partial Distortion** doesn't add a second Scar: Stress rises by 2 and the Scar stays. A **Complete Distortion** can't happen twice: the character doesn't Distort again. Instead, the player chooses one of two things, right there in the fiction: the character dies in that moment, or goes **Fallen**: a full, permanent transformation, the player retiring the character into an NPC the table might meet again. Whether they die is the player's call. What they become is not: it depends on the **self**. If the character's self is still there at the end (they held onto their Desire, their Identity phrase, a Bond), they become an **Abnormality**: unreachable but still themselves, with their Desire and Fear as its Behavior and Trigger. If the self is gone (they let it all go, or the Verdict won outright), they become a **Peccatulum** of their Alignment (Part X): a mindless thing that is only that Sin, and nothing else. The table decides which by asking what the character was holding at the end; there is no roll.

A character can still end up with more than one Scar over a campaign, if genuinely distinct, unrelated fault lines each break in turn — that's simply more than one wound, not the same wound stacking.

This is exactly why Healing matters. A Reckoning that closes out a Scar — the Reckoning is next — doesn't just lift the −1 penalty: it makes that fault line human again, safe to risk breaking a second time if the City ever pushes that hard on it again.

> **GM ADVICE**
> When a Hail Mary comes up short, you haven't been handed a debuff to apply — you've been handed a scene. Before you narrate anything, ask what the character's Distortion actually looks like, and ask what the rest of the table can do about it. If your first instinct is "how long does this last," stop and ask "what does this character need to come back" instead. That question is the whole scene.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A Backstreets medic's fault line is people she couldn't save. Begged for help she can't give, she rolls a Partial on her Hail Mary. Partial Distortion: her hands lengthen and thread through the wound like she's done this a hundred times in some worse life, something under her skin catching the light wet and wrong — and she keeps working, keeps using the patient's name, even as the thing she's become starts reaching for wounds nobody asked her to touch. Her partner grabs her by the wrist and says it plainly: *he's already gone, and you're still here.* It takes a long beat, her hands twitching like they don't want to stop, before they're just hands again. Nobody in the room forgets what they saw.

> **EXAMPLE**
> A Fixer whose fault line is control is forced to beg for his life, and rolls a Critical Failure on the Hail Mary that follows. Complete Distortion: something wearing his shape takes over — his shadow stops matching his movements, his eyes go flat and colorless, and it starts making sure nothing can ever put him in that position again, methodically, without asking his crew what they think about it. Nobody's sure if he's still in there until one of them grabs his hand mid-motion and says his name like it's the only thing she's ever been sure of — and for a moment, something in his grip hesitates. It takes the whole rest of the scene, and a promise she has no idea if she can keep, before the color comes back to his eyes. Scar — Tell: his left hand won't stop moving when he's cornered afterward, like it's still reaching for whatever let him do that. His Boundary against ever losing control in front of someone is marked Broken until he heals it.

## 7. The Reckoning

Between sessions, or during a real downtime scene, a player may declare a **Reckoning**: an actually-played scene where the character confronts their wound — a real conversation with the Bond that matters most to it, a controlled but real test of the Boundary, an honest reckoning with the Burden.

It has to be roleplayed, not simply declared. The GM judges whether the scene actually engaged the wound — one that stayed comfortable or dodged the hard part doesn't count, and a Reckoning can fail.

On a successful Reckoning, Stress drops by 1. If the scene specifically confronted the cause of a Scar, that Scar **Heals**: its Broken Boundary repairs, its penalty lifts, and it becomes pure story color rather than a standing mechanical cost.

**Integration.** A second, harder Reckoning over an already-Healed Scar — the whole table agreeing this is a genuine turning point, not routine business — grants a Permanent E.G.O. from that wound, exactly as a second Manifestation would. A character's worst moment becoming one of their greatest sources of strength should feel earned, never automatic.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Months into a campaign, Mira finally has the real conversation with Yuna about the night she froze — not the rehearsed version. The GM agrees it lands. Stress drops by 1, and from here on it's visibly a little easier for her to breathe in a crisis.

**GM Advice.** A Reckoning that always succeeds stops feeling earned. Ask yourself: did this scene cost the character something real, and was at least one other person meaningfully involved? If not, let the player try again another time, not this session.

## 8. Worked Example: Mira Sato's Hail Mary

Mira enters a session at E.G.O. 4/4, Stress 1, no Traumas beyond her starting one, Boundary intact.

Mid-session, her Trauma trigger has already fired once — Stress ticks to 2 (and her E.G.O. maximum drops to 3), hands going still. Later, Yuna is caught in the middle of something dangerous, and Mira has to decide, right now, whether to act. It hits her Boundary directly — *I will never let someone die on my table because I hesitated* — and her E.G.O. is already down to 1. Committing here would take her to 0: Hail Mary.

Resolve 4 + Medicine 3, seven dice, Difficulty 3. No Boundary broken yet, so no penalty; she spends her Bond with Yuna for an eighth die. She rolls five Successes against Difficulty 3, which is Difficulty+2: a Critical Success.

Manifestation, clean: warmth runs down both her forearms and her hands simply know exactly what to do, faster than thought. The table names the temporary shape *Steady Hands*, tied directly to her Identity phrase — "the one who fixes people, not machines." Yuna is safe. E.G.O. refills to 1. Stress rises to 3; her E.G.O. maximum stays at 3. This is her first Manifestation on this fault line; a second one, whenever it comes, makes *Steady Hands* permanent.

Weeks later, in downtime, Mira's player declares a Reckoning: she finally tells Yuna the truth about the night she froze. The GM agrees it lands. Stress drops back to 2; her E.G.O. maximum stays at 3 until Stress falls to 1.
