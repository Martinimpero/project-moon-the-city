# PART I — THE CITY

## 1. The City at a Glance

The City is enormous, old, and layered — a place where a corporate tower can stand two streets from a district the utilities forgot about. Nobody sees all of it. What follows is the shape of it, in broad strokes; Part VII gives the GM tools to fill in the specific Districts, Wings, and Syndicates your table plays in.

Four kinds of power run the City, and every character answers to at least one of them:

**The Head** sits above everything and is rarely seen. It doesn't negotiate, argue, or explain itself — it sets the Taboos every District has to answer to and steps in only when one is broken, or when something threatens the City's order directly. Everything smaller than that, it lets the City handle on its own. Nobody has ever met the Head. Everyone has felt its decisions.

**Wings** are corporations built around a Singularity — a contained piece of something not fully of this world, at the center of the company's entire business. Each Wing holds its own District: a Nest at the corporate core, and a share of Backstreets around it, both bound by that Wing's own house Taboos. A Wing pays well, protects its own, and asks for a kind of loyalty that looks a lot like family right up until it doesn't. Working for one is one of the most common ways to survive the City.

**Syndicates** run the Backstreets day to day — not because nobody owns that ground, but because the Wing that does rarely bothers to manage it directly. Syndicates run on favors, debt, and territory, and they collect on both eventually. The five most powerful are the Five Fingers, dangerous enough to rank as Stars of the City in their own right.

**Offices** are where most working Fixers actually punch in: small business outfits that take on Fixer contracts and pass the work down, answering in turn to the larger Associations above them. An Office presses through the ordinary weight of the job — quotas, cuts taken off the top, whether the person assigning work still trusts you — not through anything as clean as a form.

Between all four, there are Fixers — people hired to do the jobs too small for the Head to notice and too dangerous, illegal, or strange for anyone official to touch. Player characters are usually Fixers, though not always; the game works equally well for a Wing employee, a Syndicate runner, or an Office regular who's gotten pulled into something bigger than their desk.

And running underneath everything are Abnormalities — things that don't follow the City's rules, contained, studied, occasionally loose, and never fully understood by anyone who deals with them. See §4, below, and Part VII for how to run one.

**Numbness.** Cityfolk rarely hold funerals, keep their distance, and avoid strong ties: loss is common, and closeness is the unusual thing. Fixers die young and know it. It is why a Bond matters here more than it would anywhere else, and why a scene where someone lets themselves be close should be allowed to land.

## 2. Fixers and Associations

A Fixer takes on work nobody official wants their name attached to: recovery, negotiation, investigation, protection, removal. Fixers are licensed and graded by the **Associations**, the City's twelve formal bodies, and every completed job is reported back to them.

An **Association** is a formal body with a specialty, four regional Branches (North, East, South, West) and six Sections. It licenses Fixers, grades them and the Offices they work through, sorts the City's work, and enforces the contracts it brokers. The largest and most trusted is the **Hana Association**. Most Fixers work through an Office; an *Associate* Office takes the work it is assigned for a steady salary and **can't refuse**, while independents are free, rare, and rarely last. An Association also vouches for a Fixer to a nervous client, or warns its whole network that someone can't be trusted, and standing lost with one follows a Fixer everywhere else in the City. After every job the Fixer files a **report**; the reports are what their Grade is made of (Part VII §3).

Every Fixer, regardless of who they work through, carries a **Grade** — a number from 9 (least proven) to 1 (among the City's most capable), rising only through work that's actually been done and vouched for. Part IX covers Grade and what it changes about a character.

**When this is set.** By default the game is set after the White Nights and the Dark Days, when the City learned that people could Distort and that a few could answer it with E.G.O. Both are **recent and rare**: most Cityfolk have never seen one, the Wings and Associations are still working out what they are, and nobody trusts what they are told. Other eras work too: the years of the Smoke War, the days of L Corporation's containment facilities, or the Limbus-era scramble for Golden Boughs. The rules don't change; only what the City knows does.

> **IN THE CITY**
> Most Fixers never meet the people who ultimately benefit from their work. A contract passes through two or three intermediaries before it reaches a Fixer's hands, and by the time it does, the client's name is usually the least true thing about it.

## 3. The Four Institutions

Each of the City's major powers presses on a character differently. Knowing which kind of pressure you're under changes what you can do about it.

**The Head.** The Head never rolls dice against a player character and essentially never appears as a person at the table. It presses through consequence, and only when a Taboo is actually on the table — a Heat Clock (Part V) filling all the way, a file that goes missing overnight, an order that overrides a decision that made perfect sense five minutes earlier. It doesn't respond to danger in general; plenty of the City's violence and chaos simply isn't its problem. Run it like weather, not like an NPC — it doesn't negotiate, and it doesn't explain.

**Syndicates.** Syndicates press through Debt and Obligation — Bonds owed to people who collect on them without much institutional restraint. Standing with a Syndicate opens black-market access and a measure of protection in the Backstreets; owing one means being spent like any other Bond, just with fewer limits on how hard.

**Offices.** Offices press through the daily grind of Fixer work itself — a quota that has to be hit, a cut taken off every payment before it reaches the crew, standing with whoever hands out the next job. Losing an Office's goodwill doesn't end in paperwork. It ends in worse jobs, or none at all.

**Wings (Companies).** A Wing presses through Employment — a formal Obligation Bond that cuts both ways on purpose. It's harder than usual to spend an Employment Bond against your own Wing (real institutional protection makes that costly), but working for one grants **Wing Support**: reduced Harm severity through company medical, a small recovery bonus during sanctioned downtime, and stronger starting Bonds with coworkers. It's the deliberate, mechanized version of "this company is horrible, but the salary is incredible, and I don't know what I'd do without them." Wing Support reduces how bad an injury is; it never erases one outright.

**Associations.** Not a fifth power, but the one every Fixer answers to first. An Association presses through the license and the Report: it decides who gets work and what a Grade is worth, and a falsified Report or a broken brokered contract costs standing and opens a Heat Clock on it (Part VII §3). It never rolls against a PC.

> **EXAMPLE**
> Mira, a Wing researcher, has an Employment Obligation of 3 with her Wing. When she's hurt on shift, her Harm tier drops by one, quietly handled by internal medical — Wing Support at work. When she instead wants to use that same Bond to get herself out of a bad assignment, it costs a full point of Bond Strength, not a temporary favor. Pushing back against an institution like this always leaves a mark.

## 4. Abnormalities

An Abnormality is something that doesn't follow the City's physical or causal rules — recovered, contained, sometimes studied, sometimes simply endured. Player characters will occasionally work directly with one, more often work around one, and should almost never fight one as a first resort.

Abnormalities are not monster-of-the-week combat encounters. A well-run Abnormality is a mystery with a behavior and a trigger, resolved through investigation, care, negotiation, or sacrifice — combat is what happens when every safer approach has already failed. Part VII gives the GM a complete template for building one.

## 5. Contracts and the Shape of a Job

Most sessions start with a **Contract** — a job with a client, an objective, a payment, and a Risk rating from 1 to 4. Part V covers Contracts in full, including how to negotiate one and how to generate one on the fly. Accepting a Contract as offered is always an option. Pushing back on its terms is a scene in its own right, and pushing too hard costs standing with the client even when it works.

With the shape of the City established, Part II covers how the game actually resolves what characters do inside it.
